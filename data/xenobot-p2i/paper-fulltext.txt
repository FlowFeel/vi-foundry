ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence increasingly serves as a natural-language interface to com-
plex technical systems, letting people accomplish sophisticated tasks by describ-
ing what they want rather than specifying how to do it. Extending this interface
to living systems is harder: unlike code or images, a biological intervention has
no closed-form linguistic meaning, and the paired language-intervention-outcome
data needed to learn such a mapping is expensive to collect, since each exam-
ple requires its own wet-lab experiment. One way around this is to treat an ex-
isting archive of interventions and their already-observed outcomes as a fixed,
offline dataset, and use a vision-language model to judge, without any new exper-
iments, whether an archived outcome matches a natural-language description. But
whether that judgment is reliable enough to train a language-to-intervention map-
ping on – without new experiments and without human validation – has remained
unclear. Here we show that a natural-language interface for a living organism –
a xenobot, a synthetic multicellular construct with no nervous system – can be
learned entirely offline this way, using a vision-language model’s own judgment
as the sole training reward: an instruction is mapped to the intervention already on
record as producing the described behavior. This mapping generalizes to entirely
new instructions, evaluated against archive data withheld from training (80.0%
held-out accuracy vs. a66.7%chance baseline, matching a network trained di-
rectly on ground-truth labels). These results indicate that offline archives, com-
bined with automated judgment, can substitute for costly new experiments when
learning to ground natural language in a living system’s own recorded behavior
– an intermediate step toward directing living matter with the same ease as code,
though whether these predicted interventions actually reproduce their intended
behavior on live, newly-stimulated cells remains to be tested.
1 INTRODUCTION
Imagine a clinician or bioengineer simply describing what they want – calm this inflamed tissue,
direct these cells to close a wound, steer this synthetic biobot toward a target – and having cells,
organoids, or synthetic biobots (Kriegman et al., 2021; Blackiston et al., 2021a; Levin, 2021) carry
out that intention directly, the way a modern software system already responds to a natural-language
request. Controlling living systems this way is a long-standing goal across biology, medicine, and
synthetic biology, yet the interface remains fundamentally low-level: practitioners specify interven-
tions parameter by parameter, through trial and error informed by domain expertise. In other tech-
nical domains, natural language has emerged as a high-level interface to complex systems: users
specify a goal – generate this code (Fried et al., 2023), design this molecule (Bengio et al., 2021),
plan this trajectory (Huang et al., 2022) – and a learned model translates intent into low-level action.
This raises a question central to applied machine learning (ML): can a similar interface be learned

Two properties make this hard. First, collecting paired (prompt, intervention, outcome) data online is
prohibitively expensive: each datum is a wet-lab experiment, ruling out approaches that need a fresh
experiment per prompt or per objective. Second, the objective space is inherently multi-task and
contains opposing goals – e.g. “stop” versus “accelerate” – and a single policy trained across such
goals is known to suffer objective interference, where the gradient for one goal actively degrades
another (Yu et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2018), so naively pooling all objectives into one unconstrained
shared model is not a safe default either. Together, these rule out the two most direct approaches:
brute-forcing the mapping with enough paired data, and training one policy across all objectives
without accounting for how they interact.
We address both properties by treating language-to-intervention as an offline learning problem.
Archives of intervention-outcome pairs already exist, collected for purposes other than language-
conditioned learning; we treat such an archive as a fixed dataset over which a policy is trained
without any new experiments. To supply a reward signal in the absence of labeled prompt-outcome
alignment, we use a pretrained VLM as a zero-shot scorer (Rocamonde et al., 2023), judging how
well an archived outcome matches a natural-language description, and check that judgment against
independent, non-VLM ground truth rather than trust it by assumption. The policy uses one output
head per behavioral category over a shared backbone.
Contributions.This work makes three key contributions, each tested directly in Section 4 rather
than only asserted: (1) we demonstrate an archive-based learning paradigm for language-to-
intervention translation, using a vision-language model’s own judgment as the reward in place of
new experimental feedback; (2) we validate this approach end-to-end, generalizing to entirely new
instructions on archive data withheld from training at80.0%ground-truth accuracy, against a66.7%
chance baseline; (3) we show this VLM-derived reward is not redundant even with only two be-
haviors currently distinguished: replacing it with a single fixed duration chosen directly from real

2 RELATEDWORK
Large language models serve as general-purpose interfaces to complex domains, generating func-
tional code (Chen et al., 2021), aiding molecular design (Jumper et al., 2021), and assisting sci-
entific discovery (Zheng et al., 2025), typically letting users specify goals in language rather than
low-level parameters. Biological control is harder: language has no closed-form semantics for what
an electrical stimulus should mean, and paired (prompt, intervention, outcome) data is expensive
to collect. Synthetic biology has produced xenobots, livingXenopus laeviscell assemblies exhibit-
ing coordinated motion (Kriegman et al., 2021; Blackiston et al., 2021a), where targeted electrical
or optogenetic interventions elicit reproducible behavioral change (Zajdel et al., 2020; Levin, 2021;
Emiliani et al., 2022), but control remains manual, tuned parameter-by-parameter without a language
interface.
Vision-language models can serve as zero-shot reward models (Rocamonde et al., 2023; Radford
et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), though rarely validated against ground truth in-
dependent of the VLM itself – a gap we address directly. Learning across opposing behavioral goals
is a multi-task problem, for which task-specific heads over a shared backbone are a standard remedy
(Yu et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2018; Ruder, 2017; Caruana, 1997). Training from a fixed archive with
only terminal rewards places this work in the offline, contextual-bandit regime (Levine et al., 2020;
May et al., 2012), for which derivative-free search such as CMA-ES (Hansen & Ostermeier, 2001)
is standard; we show the reward is in fact differentiable, so both backpropagation and gradient-free
search apply, and use their agreement to validate the optimum found.
Two prior studies explore the underlying idea of prompt-to-intervention learning as a proof-of-
concept in simulated, artificial-life cells; we position this work explicitly against the specific gaps
each left open. Le et al. (2025) introduced a P2I network mapping a prompt embedding to a spatial
vector field steering simulated 2D cell collectives, scored by a VLM against a fixed one-word vo-
cabulary (e.g. “clustering” vs. “scattering”); the reward was treated as non-differentiable by design,
optimized only with(1+1)-ES and a genetic algorithm, and the paper flags gradient interference be-
tween opposing objectives as an open problem at multi-prompt scale. Le et al. (2026) extends this,
replacing the fixed vocabulary with a continuous, free-form VLM score in[0,1]– closer in spirit
to the reward used here – but trained on a single prompt (“form a cluster”), again assumed non-
differentiability, and lists gradient-trainable optimization and real-cell validation as explicit future
work. This paper answers both directly rather than by assumption: the VLM-derived reward is fit as
a smooth, differentiable function and trained by direct backpropagation, cross-validated against four
independent optimizers; we train and evaluate on 33 prompts rather than one; and every experiment
here is on a real, living, non-neural organism rather than a physics simulation.

3.2 DATASET AND TRAJECTORY EXTRACTION
The archive consists of videos of individually-tracked xenobots undergoing a three-phase protocol:
a PRE baseline period, a DURING period (an electrical stimulus of controlled durationd, the sole
intervention parameter here), and a POST period. We refer to each such recorded trial as abatchb
throughout (a full tracked trial, not an ML minibatch).
From raw video to trajectory.Extracting the trajectory used throughout this paper (Figure 2) re-
quires suppressing visual noise specific to this imaging setup – stimulus-induced air bubbles, spec-
ular reflection, and debris – that a naive brightness- or contour-based tracker confuses with the
xenobot itself. We track position per-frame using the Segment Anything Model (SAM) (Kirillov
et al., 2023) across three complementary detection strategies – bounding-box re-detection, optical-
flow-guided prompting, and grid-search prompting – each cross-checked against dish geometry and
the tracked xenobot’s own appearance to reject false positives. This yields, for each batch, a PRE-
phase and a POST-phase position trace, each a 2D trajectory in real pixel coordinates. This – not
a precomputed velocity – is what the VLM judgeJreads (Section 3.4): ifJwere instead shown a
ready-made velocity number, judging a motion prompt could reduce to comparing two numbers, and
a plain numerical rule could do that job just as well, defeating the point of testing whether a VLM
reading raw trajectories is useful at all. Section 4.3 makes this comparison directly, training against
a purely numerical, velocity-only target instead ofJ’s judgment. Frames where all three detection
tiers fail are excluded as missing data rather than treated as stillness.

3.3 ARCHITECTURE
We embed prompts with SentenceBERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), a frozen 384-dimensional
sentence encoder. The P2I policy network (Figure 1e) takes this embedding as input and outputs
a predicted intervention duration, through a fully-connected backbone:384→64(ReLU, dropout
0.2)→32(ReLU, dropout 0.2), with one sigmoid output head per behavior category (26,786param-
eters total for 2 heads) – one head per behavioral direction, so that opposing objectives (e.g. “stop”
vs. “accelerate”) never share an output. A single shared head is a real alternative, not ruled out by as-
sumption: we test it directly, with a distributional(µ,σ)output and regularization tuned specifically
for it, and it remains both worse and less stable on unseen language than 2 heads (Supplementary
Material) – so we use 2 heads throughout.

3.5 EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We verify no held-out prompt duplicates a training prompt and bound cross-split similarity (full list
in the Supplementary Material). A held-out prompt’s output head is inferred from its embedding via
nearest-centroid similarity to training-prompt centroids, never looked up from a known category.
LetP=P train⊔P test andA=A train⊔A test be the disjoint prompt and archive splits (Section 3.2).
Four evaluation cells follow from the product of prompt side×archive side:
Train=P train×Atrain,GT3=P train×Atest,GT1=P test×Atrain,GT2=P test×Atest.(4)
For a cellP ′×A′, a predictionf θ(p),p∈P ′, is scored byk-nearest-neighbor vote (k=3) among
{di :i∈A ′}against each neighbor’s true, independently-tracked ‘behavior group’ label, and is
correct if the majority vote matchesp’s intended direction. GT2 is the only cell where bothp /∈Ptrain
and everyi∈A ′ satisfiesi /∈Atrain – generalization along both axes at once.
Chance on this task is66.7%, not50%: prompts are drawn evenly from three buckets (mo-
tion increase, motion reduction, stop), but ‘stop’ and ‘motion reduction’ are both scored against
the same ‘slower’ archive direction, so two of every three prompts need ‘slower’ – a baseline that al-
ways answers ‘slower’ is right two-thirds of the time by construction, not by tracking any real signal.
Two further checks reuse this setup without retraining: an8×-paraphrase stress test (Section 4.1)
and a post-hoc sub-label split of ‘slower’ into genuine stops vs. mild slowdowns (Section 4.4).

One of the contributions we state in the introduction is that this offline paradigm generalizes to
entirely new instructions at well above chance; this section runs that experiment directly. Table 1

5 DISCUSSION ANDFUTUREWORK
In this paper, we have demonstrated that a natural-language interface for a living organism can
be learned entirely offline, from an existing archive and a vision-language model’s own judgment,
without any new experiments. The resulting P2I mapping generalizes to entirely new instructions
at80.0%ground-truth accuracy against a66.7%chance baseline, holding across three independent
optimizers sharing the same VLM reward; this reward is also not redundant with a simpler numerical
alternative, which collapses held-out generalization to59.1%, below chance (full analysis in the
Supplementary Material).
This paper’s scope has real limits: one intervention parameter, a small archive (101 of 149 tracked
batches), predefined behavior categories, and no main-text result from a new physical experi-
ment. The system retrieves rather than generates – every prediction is the archive’s own closest
already-observed match, with compositional recombination beyond it left untested (Supplementary
Material). The most direct next step is prospective: does a predicted intervention, applied to a
newly-stimulated xenobot never seen in training, actually reproduce its intended behavior? Beyond
xenobots, the same offline, archive-plus-VLM-judgment paradigm applies wherever an intervention-
outcome archive already exists – other synthetic living machines, cell and tissue cultures under elec-
trical or optogenetic control, and, further out, clinical bioelectric therapy (Ebrahimkhani & Levin,
2021; Zajdel et al., 2020; Blackiston et al., 2021b; Levin, 2021) – with this offline half a tractable
place to start.
